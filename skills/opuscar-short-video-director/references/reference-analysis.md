# Reference analysis and language-learning design

## Inspect actual material

For video references, inspect metadata, a representative contact sheet, key actions and cuts at closer spacing, plus the audio if tools permit. Do not claim to have studied a video from a filename/thumbnail. Distinguish actual playback/listening from frame/waveform analysis.

Log each reference in `REFERENCES.md`:

| Observation | Evidence/time | Transferable principle | Planned use | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Example: a reveal begins with an isolated object | reference time | detail before context builds curiosity | opening object-to-wide reveal | different composition/story |

Analyze palette/contrast, texture/brush grammar, subject scale, negative space, lighting, staging, perspective, typography, shot lengths, camera speed, transitions, animation timing, sound hierarchy and ending design. Extract principles, not the full shot sequence or another creator's distinctive characters/branding. Track reference licenses separately from inspiration; a reference link is not permission to redistribute its footage.

## Reconcile with Opuscar

Read the actual chosen style description. Save the source URL/commit and exact applicable invariants in the art bible. A gallery-only Opuscar98 style may have a published prompt rather than a `styles/<slug>` folder. Find the real source; do not fabricate one or default to an unrelated main-library style because it is convenient.

Explain internally how each reference technique fits the primary style. Examples: use a reference's reveal rhythm with gouache assets; use directional paper layers in a paper-cut style; use an appropriate documentary insert in a material-based style. Avoid mixing incompatible rendering languages without a deliberate, agreed derivative.

## Teach a learner, not just entertain

Confirm target language, learner level, accent and the single learning outcome. Make the teaching object visually identifiable. Time highlights to the spoken target expression. Separate:

- narration/subtitles;
- target-language examples;
- translation/gloss;
- scene heading or action label.

Do not show all four at full intensity together. Prefer one teaching focus, short captions and persistent examples with changing emphasis. Let the learner hear the pronunciation before a large competing effect. Preserve enough hold time to read, compare and remember; the 3–4-second visual-event rule can be met by a small underline or attention shift.

Check spelling, numerals, names, bilingual line breaks, RTL/LTR handling, font coverage and contextual meaning. Flag likely script errors for approval rather than silently teaching a correction. Do not call a grammar simplification universally true if the script/reference does not support it. Use appropriate factual sources for historical/educational claims; flags, maps and props must match the context.

Hook: show a specific puzzle, contrast or story question already supported by the supplied script. Ending: make the approved action/keyword visible and hold it long enough to act. Do not invent a worksheet, delivery promise or comment keyword absent from the script/brief.
